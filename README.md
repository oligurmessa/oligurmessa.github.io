Engineering Empathy: AI, Ethics, and the Common Good
Technology doesn't come out of nowhere. It carries the values, blind spots, and priorities of whoever built it. We're now scaling up Artificial Intelligence faster than we ever have, and that raises a hard question: how do we make sure our most powerful tools actually respect people? This is the problem that has shaped my time in school. Between my classes and my own experiences, I've started to believe the biggest issue in computer science today isn't building smarter models. It's making sure those models protect human agency and privacy. That takes more than clean math. It takes a real commitment to the common good, which happens to be one of the core ideas behind the University of St. Thomas.

I can't get there with one field alone. The way I think about this comes from looking at technology through two lenses at once: Computer Science and History. Computer Science gives me the machinery. The architecture, the math, the systems you need to build something that scales. History and Philosophy give me the rest. They tell me why we build things, who gets affected, and what goes wrong when we flatten messy human lives into clean data points.

You can see this clash most clearly when I put my machine learning work next to my East Asian history studies. For a Historical Analysis project on the Boxer Uprising, I pushed back on the standard academic framing that only cared about big-picture state power. I leaned on the historian Paul A. Cohen and argued that leaving out the psychology of 1900, especially the constant fear of death from drought and famine, gave us a distorted, almost inhuman picture of the Boxers. History taught me what Cohen calls historical empathy. It's the skill of looking past the statistics to feel the actual human reasons behind what people did.

That changed how I see myself as a programmer. In machine learning, it's scary how easy it is to treat a person as just another row in a spreadsheet or a node in a network. My history research drilled into me that the moment you abstract away the human part, you start building biased, broken systems. Whether I'm digitizing old scripts with a deep architecture like GeezNet or measuring how slow a generative model runs, I try to remember there's a real person behind every number. If an algorithm is going to serve people, it has to account for human nuance, not just averages.

Living up to that ideal in actual code is another story. It takes discipline, and I learned that the hard way. Early on, I tried to build a multi-layer neural network from scratch using only NumPy. The goal was to classify eye state and detect drowsiness. I felt good about the math, so I wrote the whole thing at once. Forward pass, backward pass, ReLU, Softmax, gradient descent, all of it in one go. Then I ran it. The model learned nothing and gradient descent blew up. The reason was obvious in hindsight. I'd bitten off way too much with no way to check my work along the way. Somewhere in my backpropagation there was a dimensionality bug in a matrix multiplication, and finding it in one giant blob of code was almost impossible.

What I took from that mess was simple. Build in pieces, and check each piece as you go. Complicated systems can't be validated at the end, whether they're mathematical or ethical. You have to test them step by step. So I tore the whole thing apart and rebuilt it, unit-testing each layer's forward and backward pass against known PyTorch outputs before I let anything touch anything else. The new version hit 92% accuracy. That whole failure-to-fix arc stuck with me, because ethical AI needs the exact same care. You can't ship a giant black box and just hope it's safe. You have to bake in interpretability and privacy at every layer.

That mix of technical care and actual empathy came together in HallPals. I was a Resident Advisor in Ireland Hall, and I kept noticing that RAs had no good central place to handle duty operations and incident reports. Everything was scattered. Reporting was a mess, responses were slow, and shift handoffs fell through the cracks, which ended up hurting the students we were supposed to be helping. So I built something. I used modern web development tools to put together an AI-assisted duty platform for RAs. Since student data is sensitive, I designed the backend around strict, privacy-first role-based access from the start. We piloted it with 10 RAs and, after a few rounds of feedback, saved roughly 30 minutes per shift. HallPals wasn't just a full-stack exercise for me. It was a small piece of digital equity, giving residential staff the tools to actually take care of their communities.

My capstone, Project Greenstep Employee, went in a more environmental direction. Our team built a clean, simple dashboard to replace the clunky spreadsheets companies usually rely on, so employees could log eco-friendly actions and watch their impact add up in real time. It showed me something I keep coming back to. When you design for clarity and accessibility, technology can actually shift people's behavior for the better.

Some of what I understand about the common good didn't happen at a keyboard at all. Volunteering at Second Harvest Heartland was a reminder of the sheer physical work behind helping a community. I spent shifts on an assembly line repackaging bulk produce for local food shelves, and the challenge there was real. Move fast, keep quality high, and get it done before the food spoils. Working shoulder to shoulder with a diverse crew to turn raw produce into dignified meals reinforced what I believe about structural equity. Honestly, the logistics of feeding people aren't that different from the logistics of data architecture. Both come down to coordinated, community-minded work that gets resources to the people who need them.

As I get ready to graduate from St. Thomas and move into the fast-track M.S. program, I'm bringing all of this with me. School taught me that being a computer scientist isn't just about writing elegant code. It's about owning the systems you put out into the world. Everything I struggled through, from chasing a matrix bug for hours to sitting with the ethical weight of historical trauma, built a sense of who I want to be: someone who's both technically sharp and willing to take a stand.

Going forward, I want to keep sharpening both sides, the technical and the human. I'm building real applications like SoulSpect and digging into advanced AI research, with a steady focus on privacy and on-device processing. I'm especially curious about mechanistic interpretability and sub-agent orchestration. St. Thomas didn't just prepare me to join the AI wave. It prepared me to help steer it. By always asking not only "can we build this?" but "should we, and who does it actually serve?", I think I'm ready to build things that protect people's dignity, respect their privacy, and genuinely do some good.
