Holistic Signature Work Reflection: Engineering Empathy—Navigating the Intersection of Artificial Intelligence, Ethics, and the Common Good
Technology does not exist in a vacuum; it is a direct reflection of the values, biases, and priorities of its creators. As we enter an era defined by the rapid scaling of Artificial Intelligence, we face an unprecedented challenge: ensuring that our most powerful computational tools are deployed ethically, sustainably, and in service of human dignity. This is the problem that has defined my academic journey. Through my coursework and lived experiences, I have come to realize that the most pressing issue in modern computer science is not simply how to build more capable models, but how to ensure these models preserve human agency and digital privacy. Addressing this requires more than just algorithmic optimization; it requires a rigorous commitment to the common good—a core pillar of the University of St. Thomas mission.

To tackle the ethical deployment of AI, I have learned that I cannot rely on a single discipline. My understanding of this theme has been profoundly shaped by viewing technology through the intersecting lenses of Computer Science and History. Computer Science provides the mechanics—the architecture, the mathematics, and the systems required to build scalable tools. However, History and Philosophy provide the human context. They teach us why we build, who is affected, and what happens when we reduce complex human experiences to mere data points.

This interdisciplinary synthesis is most evident when contrasting my deeply technical machine learning research with my studies in East Asian history. For my Historical Analysis project on the Boxer Uprising, I utilized the discipline of History to critique standard academic frameworks that focused solely on macro-level state power. Drawing on the work of historian Paul A. Cohen, I argued that omitting the psychological realities of 1900—the widespread "death anxiety" caused by severe drought and famine—resulted in a skewed, dehumanized portrayal of the Boxers. The discipline of History taught me the critical concept of "historical empathy": the ability to look past macro-statistics to understand the deeply human, emotional realities driving behavior.

This historical lens fundamentally shifted my perspective as a computer scientist. In machine learning, it is dangerously easy to treat human beings merely as rows in a dataset or nodes in a neural network. My historical research taught me that when we abstract away the human element, we build flawed, biased systems. Whether digitizing historical scripts through deep architectures like GeezNet or evaluating the latency of generative models, I now approach data with the understanding that there is a lived human reality behind every metric. Algorithms must account for human nuances, not just macro-statistics, if they are to serve the common good.

However, realizing this ideal in software development requires rigorous, disciplined execution. Early in my computer science education, I experienced a critical failure that reshaped how I approach problem-solving and accountability. For a course project, I attempted to build a multi-layer neural network from scratch using only NumPy for eye-state classification to detect drowsiness. Confident in my understanding of the mathematics, I attempted to write the forward pass, backward pass, ReLU, and Softmax gradient descent all at once. When I compiled the code, the model completely failed to learn, and gradient descent diverged. The context was clear: I was trying to do too much without verifiable checks. What didn't go well was a hidden dimensionality bug in my backpropagation matrix multiplication that was nearly impossible to isolate in a monolithic codebase.

What I learned from this failure was the critical importance of modularity and incremental verification. I realized that complex systems—whether mathematical or ethical—cannot be validated after the fact; they must be tested at every discrete step. I changed my entire development philosophy, rewriting the codebase to unit-test each layer's forward and backward pass individually against known PyTorch outputs before integration. The final model successfully converged to 92% accuracy. This "failure → learning → change" experience taught me that ethical AI development requires the same modular rigor. We cannot build massive, "black box" models and hope they are safe; we must apply mechanistic interpretability and privacy-first design at every layer of the architecture.

This synthesis of technical rigor and human-centered empathy culminated in the development of HallPals, a project where my technical and soft skills deeply complemented one another. The situation arose during my time as a Resident Advisor in Ireland Hall. I observed that RAs needed a more efficient, centralized platform for duty operations and incident management. The primary obstacle was that incident reporting procedures were highly fragmented, leading to slow response times and inefficient shift handoffs that ultimately impacted student wellbeing. Taking action, I applied modern web development practices to engineer an AI-assisted RA duty platform. Because I understood the sensitive nature of student data, I designed the backend architecture with strict, privacy-first role-based access controls. The result was a successful pilot with 10 RAs, achieving a roughly 30-minute efficiency gain per shift based on iterative user feedback. HallPals was not just an exercise in full-stack development; it was a tangible application of digital equity, ensuring that residential staff had the tools necessary to support their communities efficiently and securely.

Similarly, my capstone project, Project Greenstep Employee, allowed me to apply my skills to environmental sustainability. By building a sleek, minimal UI/UX dashboard that replaces cumbersome spreadsheets, our team empowered corporate employees to log eco-friendly actions and track their real-time impact. This project demonstrated that when technology is designed with accessibility and clarity in mind, it can drive systemic behavioral change and promote ecological stewardship.

My understanding of the common good has also been shaped far away from a computer screen. Volunteering at Second Harvest Heartland provided a visceral reminder of the physical logistics required to support a community. Working on an assembly line to repackage bulk produce for local food shelves presented a distinct challenge: maintaining high-speed efficiency and strict quality control before perishable items spoiled. Collaborating with a diverse team to turn raw agriculture into dignified nutritional support reinforced my belief in structural equity. The logistical challenges of food distribution mirror the logistical challenges of data architecture; both require coordinated, community-focused efforts to ensure resources reach those who need them most.

As I prepare to graduate from the University of St. Thomas and transition into the fast-track M.S. program, I carry these interdisciplinary lessons with me. My education has taught me that being a computer scientist is not just about writing elegant code; it is about taking responsibility for the systems I introduce into the world. The challenges I faced—from untangling matrix dimensionality bugs to navigating the ethical complexities of historical trauma—have forged a professional identity rooted in both technical excellence and moral conviction.

Looking ahead, I plan to continue developing both my technical and interpersonal skill sets as I build real-world applications like SoulSpect and dive deeper into advanced AI research. I am committed to exploring mechanistic interpretability and sub-agent orchestration with a relentless focus on privacy and on-device processing. St. Thomas has equipped me not just to participate in the AI revolution, but to actively shape it. By continually asking not just "can we build it?" but "should we build it, and who does it serve?", I am prepared to engineer solutions that protect human dignity, elevate user privacy, and powerfully advance the common good.
